## 1. Facial symmetry and attractiveness
- **Rhodes G (2006).** The evolutionary psychology of facial beauty. *Annu Rev Psychol* 57:199–226. DOI 10.1146/annurev.psych.57.102904.190208. **[not retrievable; snippet only via citing papers]**: later papers report Rhodes' mean effect size for averageness–attractiveness as 0.52 and describe her symmetry effects as "small-to-medium"; the symmetry r itself could not be verified.
- **Van Dongen S (2011).** Associations between asymmetry and human attractiveness: possible direct effects of asymmetry and signatures of publication bias. *Ann Hum Biol* 38(3):317–323. DOI 10.3109/03014460.2010.544676. **[snippet only, abstract]**: "substantial publication bias"; correcting it reduces effect sizes by 30–50 %; among the few large studies (n > 100) effect sizes were "nearly zero".
- **Jones AL, Jaeger B (2019).** Biological bases of beauty revisited. *Symmetry* 11(2):279. DOI 10.3390/sym11020279. **[snippet only]**: symmetry had a small effect, averageness a large one; machine-learning models predicted attractiveness from shape averageness, dimorphism and skin texture, not shape symmetry.
- **Lee P et al. (2025).** Further evidence that averageness and femininity, rather than symmetry and masculinity, predict facial attractiveness judgments. *Sci Rep* 15:5498. DOI 10.1038/s41598-025-86974-0. **[verified from page]**: 100 faces, 400 raters (α = 0.99); distinctiveness −0.255 (p < 0.001), asymmetry −0.092 (p = 0.18, n.s.), masculinity in male faces n.s., femininity in female faces positive; "these effects were quite weak".
- **Kleisner K et al. (2024).** Distinctiveness and femininity, rather than symmetry and masculinity, affect facial attractiveness across the world. *Evol Hum Behav* 45(1):82–90. DOI 10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2023.10.001. **[snippet only]**: 1,550 faces, 10 populations; "symmetry has no robust effect".

Net: symmetry is at best a small, publication-bias-inflated predictor; large multivariate, cross-cultural datasets find no independent effect.

## 2. Averageness vs. harmony vs. single features
- **Langlois & Roggman (1990).** Attractive faces are only average. *Psychol Sci* 1:115–121. **[snippet only]**: composites rated more attractive than almost all component faces.
- **DeBruine et al. (2007).** Dissociating averageness and attractiveness. *J Exp Psychol Hum Percept Perform* 33(6):1420–1430. **[snippet only]**: caricaturing attractive faces away from the average made them less average but more attractive — averageness is not sufficient.
- **Komori, Kawamura, Ishihara (2009).** Averageness or symmetry? *Acta Psychol* 131(2):136–142. **[snippet only]**: male faces: both; female faces: only averageness.
- Lee 2025 / Kleisner 2024 (above): a *global* configuration (distance from the population mean) predicts attractiveness weakly; single-feature "ideals" do not.

## 3. Facial width-to-height ratio (FWHR)
- **Geniole SN et al. (2015).** Evidence from meta-analyses of the FWHR as an evolved cue of threat. *PLoS ONE* 10(7):e0132726. **[verified from page]**: sex difference d = 0.11; masculinity judgments r = .35; BMI r = .31; threat behaviour in men r = .16; perceived threat r ≈ .46–.48; perceived dominance r ≈ .20–.29; **attractiveness r = −.26 (k = 14, 95 % CI −.40 to −.10)**.
- **Haselhuhn, Ormiston, Wong (2015).** Men's FWHR predicts aggression: a meta-analysis. *PLoS ONE* 10(4):e0122637. **[verified from page]**: r = .11; facial height measured "upper lip to mid-brow" or to the highest point of the eyelids — definitions differ between studies.
- **Kosinski M (2017).** FWHR does not predict self-reported behavioral tendencies. *Psychol Sci* 28(11):1675–1682. **[snippet only]**: 137,163 participants — no substantial links.
- **Valentine et al. (2014).** Judging a man by the width of his face. *Psychol Sci* 25(3):806–811. **[snippet only]**: higher FWHR → perceived dominance and short-term (not long-term) attractiveness.
- **Kramer RSS (2017).** Sexual dimorphism of FWHR in human skulls and faces. *Evol Hum Behav* 38:414–420. **[snippet only]**: little support for dimorphism in faces.
- Typical values: Carré & McCormick 2008 means ≈ 1.86 men / 1.80 women **[snippet only, secondhand]**; Třebický 2016 **[verified]** measured 1.70–1.80 depending on lens. A "typical 1.8–2.0" range is not confirmed by any primary source opened.

## 4. Neoclassical canons / Farkas
- **Farkas et al. (1985).** Revision of neoclassical canons. *Plast Reconstr Surg* 75(3):328–338. **[not retrievable]**.
- **Jayaratne et al. (2012).** Are neoclassical canons valid for Southern Chinese faces? *PLoS ONE* 7(12):e52593. **[verified from page]**: three-section (equal thirds) canon 0 %, orbital 0 %, orbitonasal 19.4 %, naso-oral 8.7 %; "should not be used as esthetic goals". Its Table 4 tabulates North American whites (from Farkas, Forrest & Litsas 2000): **equal thirds 0 %** (lower third > middle third in 100 %), orbital 33.0 %, orbitonasal 40.8 %, naso-oral 20.4 %; African Americans 0 %/13 %/3 %/1 %.
- **Le, Farkas et al. (2002).** Proportionality in Asian and North American Caucasian faces. *Aesthetic Plast Surg* 26:64–69. **[snippet only]**: deviation from canons ≈ 90–100 % in Asians vs 13–48 % in Caucasians.
- **Al-Sebaei MO (2015).** Validity of three neo-classical canons in young adults from the Arabian Peninsula. *Head Face Med* 11:4. **[verified from page]**: thirds unequal (p < 0.0001); orbital canon valid in 29 % men / 25 % women.
- **Khoshab et al. (2022).** Historical tools of anthropometric facial assessment. *Aesthet Surg J* 42(1):NP1–NP10. **[snippet only]**: no group consistently approximated the canons or Phi.

Conclusion: equal thirds essentially never occur; equal-fifths-type canons hold in a minority (≈ 20–40 % of Caucasians, far less elsewhere) and are unrelated to rated attractiveness.

## 5. Golden ratio / phi
- **Holland E (2008).** Marquardt's Phi mask: pitfalls. *Aesthetic Plast Surg* 32(2):200–208. **[snippet only]**: mask fits "masculinized white women as seen in fashion models", ill-suited for other populations; method "faulty".
- **Kiekens et al. (2008).** Putative golden proportions as predictors of facial esthetics in adolescents. *Am J Orthod Dentofacial Orthop* 134(4):480–483. **[snippet only]**: only 4 of 19 golden proportions correlated with ratings.
- **Pallett, Link, Lee (2010).** New "golden" ratios for facial beauty. *Vision Res* 50(2):149–154. **[snippet only]**: optimal eye–mouth distance ≈ 36 % of face length, eye separation ≈ 46 % of face width — the average face, not 1.618.

## 6. Canthal tilt
- **Bashour & Geist (2007).** Is medial canthal tilt a powerful cue for facial attractiveness? *Ophthal Plast Reconstr Surg* 23(1):52–56. **[snippet only]**: Photoshop-accentuated *medial* canthal tilt preferred 93 % of the time; manipulated female faces only.
- **Kempa et al. (2025).** Impact of eyebrow and lateral canthal height on perceived character traits (2,081 participants). *J Plast Reconstr Aesthet Surg* 104:417–425. **[snippet only]**: elevating the lateral canthus *reduced* attractiveness and increased dominance/threat — "more positive tilt = more attractive" is not supported.
- **Gao et al. (2025).** Racial and sexual differences of eyebrow and eyelid morphology. *Quant Imaging Med Surg* 15(1):882–897. **[verified from page]**: 2-D canthal tilt ≈ 6.0° (Chinese women), 4.9° (Chinese men), 2.4° (Caucasian women), 0.8° (Caucasian men) upward slant. Snippet-only norms: South Indians ≈ 4.7–7°, Koreans ≈ 8.5–8.8°, Asian eyelids 7.9 ± 2.4° / 8.8 ± 2.3°. Tilt decreases with age; strong ethnic differences.

## 7. Soft-tissue cephalometric norms (lateral view)
- **Legan & Burstone (1980)** **[norms snippet only]**: facial convexity 12° ± 4°; nasolabial angle 102° ± 8°; G–Sn/Sn–Me′ = 1.0; lower face–throat angle 100° ± 7°.
- **Holdaway (1983)** *Am J Orthod* 84(1):1–28 **[norms snippet only]**: H-angle ideal 10°, range 7–15° (some sources 7–14°); H-angle must rise with skeletal convexity.
- **Merrifield (1966)** *Am J Orthod* 52(11):804–822 **[snippet only]**: normal Z-angle 80° in adults, 78° at 11–15 years; the "± 9°" could not be verified (another secondary source gives 75° ± 5°).
- **Powell & Humphreys (1984)** textbook **[not retrievable; snippet only]**: nasofrontal 115–130° (some 115–135°), nasofacial 30–40° (some 36–40°), nasomental 120–132° (some 120–130°), mentocervical 80–95°; derived from attractive white faces.
- **Ellenbogen & Karlin (1980)** *Plast Reconstr Surg* 66(6):826–837 **[snippet only]**: cervicomental angle 105–120°; other reviews cite 90° or 95–125°, Halani et al. 2025 propose ≈ 100° for young Caucasian women.

## 8. Reliability of attractiveness ratings
- **Langlois et al. (2000).** Maxims or myths of beauty? *Psychol Bull* 126(3):390–423. **[snippet only]**: effective reliability of pooled ratings r = .90; cross-cultural agreement r = .94.
- **Hönekopp (2006).** *J Exp Psychol Hum Percept Perform* 32(2):199–209. **[snippet only]**: private taste about as powerful as shared taste; individual retest ≈ 0.73.
- **Kramer, Mileva, Ritchie (2018).** Inter-rater agreement in trait judgements from faces. *PLoS ONE* 13(8):e0202655. **[verified from page]**: with 10 raters Cronbach's α for attractiveness = 0.93 while individual retest r = 0.78 and shared taste ≈ 37–69 %; α says nothing about single raters.
- **Hehman et al. (2018/2025).** Assessing the point at which averages are stable. **[snippet only]**: ≈ 20–30 raters per face for a stable mean.
- **Kaschel & Hildebrandt (2023).** Can beauty be measured with photos? *Int Rev Soc Psychol* 36:5. **[verified from page]**: static–dynamic correlation r = 0.70 (CI 0.52–0.81).

## 9. Perspective / focal-length distortion
- **Ward, Ward, Fried, Paskhover (2018).** Nasal distortion in short-distance photographs. *JAMA Facial Plast Surg* 20(4):333–335. **[snippet in this pass; full text verified in the verification pass]**: at 12 in the nasal base appears ≈ 30 % (men) / 29 % (women) wider relative to bizygomatic width.
- **Bryan, Perona, Adolphs (2012).** Perspective distortion from interpersonal distance is an implicit visual cue for social judgments of faces. *PLoS ONE* 7(9):e45301. **[verified from page]**: faces shot at 45 cm rated less attractive (d = 0.31), less trustworthy and competent; FWHR is *smaller* in close photos.
- **Třebický et al. (2016).** Focal length affects depicted shape and perception of facial images. *PLoS ONE* 11(2):e0149313. **[verified from page]**: 50 mm images least attractive; FWHR women 1.716 (50 mm) → 1.797 (85/105 mm); men 1.695 → 1.787 → 1.803.
- **Derakhshan et al. (2024).** Quantifying facial distortion in modern digital photography. *Laryngoscope*. **[snippet only]**: midface vertically stretched 12–19 % at 8 in; smartphone 18 % at 8 in and 12 % at 12 in.

## 10. Ethics / bias of automated attractiveness scoring
- **Levin S (2016).** The Guardian on Beauty.AI (≈ 6,000 entrants, 44 winners, one dark-skinned). **[snippet only]**.
- **Buolamwini & Gebru (2018).** Gender Shades. *PMLR* 81. **[snippet only]**: error up to 34.7 % for darker-skinned women vs 0.8 % for lighter-skinned men.
- **Riccio & Oliver (2022); Riccio et al. (2024).** Racial bias in the beautyverse / "Mirror, mirror on the wall". **[snippet only]**: AR-beautified faces of every race are more often classified as White.
- **Hamel et al. (2025)** arXiv:2509.24138 **[snippet only]**: SCUT-FBP5500 = 4,000 Asian + 1,500 Caucasian faces; error distributions differ by ethnicity. **Obwegeser et al. (2024)** *Orthod Craniofac Res* **[snippet only]**: CNN scorer inflated scores and was biased by expression.

## Consequences for a landmark-based rating app
**Supported (modest effects):** averageness/low distinctiveness relative to a same-sex, same-population mean; femininity of shape in female faces (masculinity in male faces null). FWHR is a cue of perceived dominance/threat and *negatively* related to rated attractiveness (r = −.26); it does not predict personality.
**Weakly supported / ambiguous:** symmetry (small, inflated by publication bias, near zero in large samples); canthal tilt (mild positive tilt is the norm, ethnic baselines differ by several degrees, no evidence for an "ideal" angle, raising the lateral canthus lowers attractiveness); soft-tissue cephalometric norms (clinical population norms for white adults, not attractiveness predictors; several bounds disagree between sources).
**Unsupported or contradicted:** golden ratio/phi masks; neoclassical canons as beauty criteria (equal thirds in 0 % of measured adults); any fixed "ideal" FWHR; single-rater or single-algorithm scores presented as objective (shared taste explains ~37–69 % of variance; stable means need ≈ 20–30 raters).
**Not measurable from a single frontal photo:** every profile angle; true 3-D proportions at unknown focal length/distance (nose width +30 % at selfie distance, midface +12–19 %, FWHR −0.1); dynamic cues; population-specific norms are needed because landmark ratios differ by ethnicity and sex.
